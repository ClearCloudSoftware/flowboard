How to read, and drive, the monitor

The monitor answers four questions: does anything want me, is it progressing or stuck, am I saturated, and what is this costing. Everything on the screen exists to answer one of those. This guide names every mark and every button, and — more usefully — explains when a task moves on its own and when it is waiting for you.

Anatomy

Five stacked bands, fixed in place. Only the middle one scrolls. Reading down the screen is reading from most urgent to least.

The one rule of the layout: colour only appears where something is happening or something is wanted. When nothing is blocked, band 3 collapses to a single white line and every trace of amber leaves the screen. If you see amber, a human is the bottleneck.

The status vocabulary

Two alphabets. The row icon tells you about the task as a whole. The flow bar tells you about each of its steps. They are rendered here exactly as they appear on the screen.

Task status — the icon at the start of a row

A task shows the most demanding thing true about it. Anything blocked outranks anything running, which outranks anything finished.

Step state — each cell of the flow bar

Five cells, left to right, one per step of the flow. The third cell is split in two because build and tests run side by side.

rschplanb∥trevsum

Read that bar as: research and plan are finished, build is running while tests has already passed, review is queued behind them, summarize hasn't started.

Hover any cell on the real screen and it tells you the step, the model, how long it ran, what it cost, and how many attempts it took. The bar is a summary; the tooltip is the detail; clicking the row is the full story.

When a task moves, and why

Nothing on this screen moves because you dragged it. A task's position is decided entirely by the engine following the flow, with exactly four exceptions — the four decisions that are yours: approve, send back, answer, retry or stop.

Why you can't drag a card. Everything past the backlog is driven by the engine, so dragging a task into review would be a lie — no review has run. The board shows where work is, not where you'd like it to be. Your leverage is the four decisions above, and the flow itself.

Every action, and what it costs you

Grouped by where you'll find it. The changes column is what actually happens to the task; the money column is the part people get surprised by.

Answering is free; stopping is not. A run that asks you a question saves its place and stops billing while it waits — leaving a question unanswered costs nothing but time. A run you stop, or one that fails, has already spent its money, and that spend is re-labelled wasted rather than refunded.

The three numbers in the topbar

What an hour actually costs

Each step names a model, and the model sets the rate. Four agents at once is not a small number — that's the point of showing it.

Wasted is the number to attack. It's every dollar spent on an attempt that was thrown away — a failed build, a run you stopped, a review you sent back. A healthy board keeps it under roughly a tenth of the bill. If it's climbing, the recipe is usually the culprit, not the code: check the Pipeline view for a thick retry loop.
